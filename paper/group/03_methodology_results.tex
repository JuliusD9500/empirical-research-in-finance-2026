\section*{Methodology and Results}

% (b) Baseline regression
We begin with the baseline model in task (b), using one-year-ahead excess return as the dependent variable and book-to-market as the sole explanatory variable. Pooled OLS combines all firm-year observations in one regression. Every specification uses the same 2,664 observations, so changes in the results reflect changes in the model rather than the sample. The baseline book-to-market coefficient is positive and significantly different from zero (0.088, $t=12.920$, $p<0.001$). A one-unit higher ratio is associated with an 8.762-percentage-point higher excess return in the following year.

\newpage
% (c) Full model with controls
To assess whether this relationship also reflects other firm characteristics, model (c) adds size, leverage, profitability and dividend yield. For firm $i$ in year $t$, the model is
\begin{equation}
r^e_{i,t+1}=\alpha+\beta_B \mathrm{B2M}_{it}+\beta_S Size_{it}
+\beta_L Lev_{it}+\beta_P Prof_{it}+\beta_D DY_{it}+\varepsilon_{it}.
\label{eq:controls}
\end{equation}
Here, $r^e$ is excess return, $\mathrm{B2M}$ is book-to-market, and $Size$, $Lev$, $Prof$ and $DY$ denote size, leverage, profitability and dividend yield. The intercept is $\alpha$ and $\varepsilon$ is the error term. Returns and financial ratios enter in decimal form. The book-to-market coefficient falls to 0.059 but remains significant ($p<0.001$), suggesting that the baseline relationship partly overlaps with the added characteristics. Size is negatively associated with returns ($p<0.001$), while profitability is positively associated ($p=0.036$). Leverage and dividend yield are not individually significant at 5\% ($p=0.532$ and $p=0.115$). We retain them to account for financing and payout differences when estimating the other coefficients. A non-significant individual test does not establish that a variable is irrelevant as a control. Table~\ref{tab:regressions} consolidates all regression results.

% (d) Different slopes for small and large firms
Task (d) asks whether the value effect differs between small and large firms. We replace continuous size with $Small_{it}$, which equals one when market capitalisation is below the full-sample median of EUR 44.917 million and zero otherwise. This common threshold is calculated across all firm-year observations, so a firm can change classification over time. Table~\ref{tab:small_firms} reports the annual counts.

\input{group/tables/04_small_firms}

The dummy captures a difference in return levels, while its interaction with book-to-market allows a different slope for small firms. We estimate
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r^e_{i,t+1}={}&\alpha+\beta_B \mathrm{B2M}_{it}+\delta Small_{it}
+\gamma(\mathrm{B2M}_{it}\times Small_{it})\\
&+\beta_L Lev_{it}+\beta_P Prof_{it}+\beta_D DY_{it}+\varepsilon_{it}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:interaction}
\end{equation}

% Keep the hypotheses and their interpretation together on one page.
\begin{samepage}
The book-to-market slope is $\beta_B$ for large firms and $\beta_B+\gamma$ for small firms. The null hypothesis $H_0\colon\gamma=0$ therefore states that the slopes are equal, while $H_1\colon\gamma\neq0$ states that they differ. We reject the null ($t=3.252$, $p=0.001$). A one-unit higher book-to-market ratio is associated with returns 4.259 percentage points higher among large firms and 8.874 points higher among small firms. The difference of 4.615 points indicates an economically stronger value effect among small firms, although the regression cannot distinguish risk compensation from mispricing.
\par
\end{samepage}

% (e) Joint test of no relationship in either size group
Having established a difference between the slopes, task (e) tests whether book-to-market is unrelated to returns in both groups. This requires the joint null $H_0\colon\beta_B=\gamma=0$, against $H_1\colon\beta_B\neq0$ or $\gamma\neq0$. Comparing equation~\eqref{eq:interaction} with a restricted model excluding both book-to-market terms gives $F(2,2657)=47.512$ ($p<0.001$). We reject the joint null. This agrees with the positive relationship in model (c), which imposed one common book-to-market slope. The interaction model shows that this common slope conceals a stronger relationship among small firms. The restricted model and test are included in the Appendix.

% (f) Year and industry dummies
Differences across calendar years and industries could also influence the estimated relationship. Model (f) therefore adds year effects $\lambda_t$ and two-digit SIC industry effects $\eta_{s(i,t)}$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r^e_{i,t+1}={}&\alpha+\beta_B \mathrm{B2M}_{it}+\delta Small_{it}
+\gamma(\mathrm{B2M}_{it}\times Small_{it})\\
&+\beta_L Lev_{it}+\beta_P Prof_{it}+\beta_D DY_{it}
+\lambda_t+\eta_{s(i,t)}+\varepsilon_{it}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dummies}
\end{equation}
Year effects capture common annual shocks, while industry effects capture average return differences across industries. The model includes 11 year dummies and nine industry dummies, with 2010 and SIC 20 as the respective reference categories. Both book-to-market coefficients remain close to column (d)/(e) and significant at 1\%. Adjusted $R^2$ rises from 0.097 to 0.343, and the added dummies are jointly significant ($F(20,2637)=50.607$, $p<0.001$). This supports the richer specification because it explains more return variation even after accounting for the additional regressors. It does not, however, establish a causal effect of valuation.

% (g) Clustered inference and economic interpretation
Finally, column (g) uses HC1 standard errors clustered by firm. This allows the error variance to differ across observations and errors for the same firm to be correlated over time. Coefficients and model fit are unchanged because clustering changes only the estimated uncertainty. Both book-to-market terms remain significant at 1\%. Profitability is positive and significant ($\hat\beta_P=0.131$, $t=2.335$, $p=0.020$). Using the unrounded estimate, a ten-percentage-point increase in profitability corresponds to $\hat\beta_P\times0.100\times100\approx1.311$ percentage points higher subsequent excess returns, holding the other regressors fixed.
