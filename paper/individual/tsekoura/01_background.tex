\section*{Background}

Hurricane Helene made landfall in Florida's Big Bend region on 26 September 2024 and then moved inland. It caused about \$78.7 billion in damage across Georgia, South Carolina, North Carolina, Tennessee and Virginia. Different from many hurricanes, most of the destruction was not on the coast but inland, in counties where very few households have flood insurance, so most families and local firms had to cover the losses themselves. Because some counties were hit very hard while areas close by were almost untouched, Helene is a good case to compare firms that were directly exposed with firms that were not.

Dessaint and Matray (2017) find that US firms in counties hit by a hurricane hold more cash afterwards, even when their business did not really get worse. They explain this by salience: after managers experience a disaster, the risk feels more real and they become more careful. This proposal uses the same idea but looks at R\&D instead. R\&D is long-term and risky, and it is hard to finance with debt or to stop and restart, so it is likely to be cut first if managers become more risk-averse. The research question is: does direct exposure to Hurricane Helene cause firms to reduce R\&D investment relative to unexposed firms, and is this decline accompanied by a precautionary increase in cash holdings?
